Navigating Social Mobility: Intra-generational and Inter-generational Perspectives

Last updated on:

January 11, 2026

Where you end up in life – your job, your income, your social standing – is it purely a result of your own effort, or does it depend on where you started? This question sits at the heart of social mobility, one of the most debated concepts in sociology. Social mobility refers to the movement of individuals or groups within a social hierarchy, typically measured through changes in occupation, income, or education. That movement can happen within a single lifetime or across generations – and the difference between these two timescales tells us a great deal about how open, or closed, a society really is.

What social mobility actually means

At its core, social mobility captures whether people can change their position in the class structure. The shift can be upward or downward, and it is not always easy to judge whether a change is entirely positive or negative. Russian-American sociologist Pitirim Sorokin, who first introduced the concept formally, argued that no society is completely open – where anyone can move freely – nor completely closed, like a rigid caste system. Every society sits somewhere in between, and the pace of mobility can vary across different historical periods depending on how developed that society is.

Social mobility typically refers to vertical mobility – movement up or down the socioeconomic scale. But it can also be horizontal, meaning a shift between positions of similar status (for example, moving from one professional occupation to another of equal prestige without gaining or losing class standing). Most sociological interest, however, centres on vertical movement, since this is where real inequality either tightens or loosens.

Intra-generational mobility: movement within a lifetime

Intra-generational mobility refers to changes in an individual’s social position over the course of their own life. Sociologists measure it by comparing an individual’s status at multiple points across their life, such as early career versus mid-career versus retirement. This type of mobility reflects how personal effort, career decisions, and broader economic conditions interact to shape a person’s trajectory.

Consider someone who enters the workforce as a warehouse operative and, through gaining professional qualifications in their thirties, moves into a management role. Their class position has shifted – within a single lifetime. This is vertical intra-generational mobility. Contrast this with someone who moves from one administrative job to another of similar pay and status – that is horizontal intra-generational mobility, with no real class change.

What drives intra-generational change?

Several forces shape whether and how much an individual’s position shifts during their lifetime. Career progression through promotion or entrepreneurship is one route. Economic downturns, job losses, health problems, or periods of automation can also trigger downward intra-generational mobility – particularly for workers whose skills become obsolete as industries restructure. Research published in American Sociological Review found that intra-generational occupational mobility in the United States increased significantly between 1969 and 2011, driven by changing labour force composition, new technologies, and shifting employment relations.

Importantly, downward mobility in one’s own career tends to be particularly harmful to subjective well-being – more so than downward inter-generational mobility – because it combines both the disadvantages of a lower class position and the psychological experience of a declining trajectory.

Inter-generational mobility: movement across generations

Inter-generational mobility looks at differences in social position between parents and their children. It is measured by comparing the occupations, incomes, and educational levels of parents and their children. A high degree of inter-generational mobility is widely considered a sign of equality of opportunity – an indicator that a society rewards effort rather than inherited advantage.

When the daughter of a factory worker becomes a surgeon, or the son of a secondary school teacher earns significantly more than his parents ever did, inter-generational upward mobility has occurred. The reverse – where children end up in lower-status positions than their parents – represents downward inter-generational mobility, and it raises important questions about structural decline and deindustrialisation in particular regions.

Key factors shaping inter-generational outcomes

Research consistently shows that inter-generational mobility is shaped by several interconnected forces. Parental education, income, and family structure are all strong predictors of children’s educational and occupational outcomes. Children with professionally-educated parents tend to have access to better-resourced schools, cultural experiences, and social networks – giving them a structural head start. Discrimination also matters: research on hiring practices has shown that identical job applications receive more callbacks when associated with names that sound “white”, illustrating how racial discrimination functions as a structural barrier that limits mobility for entire groups.

Social networks – the professional and personal connections a family maintains – also play a significant role. Those with links to higher-status occupational circles are better placed to provide opportunities for the next generation, reinforcing advantage across family lines.

Comparing the two: timescales and drivers

While both types of mobility reflect social change, they operate differently. Intra-generational mobility is shaped largely by personal choices, career trajectories, and economic conditions that affect individuals during their lifetimes. Inter-generational mobility is more structural – it reflects whether the broader systems of education, employment, and welfare are genuinely open or whether advantage and disadvantage are quietly passed down. Circulation mobility describes movement attributable to an individual’s own traits and efforts, while structural mobility describes movement caused by changes in the social environment itself – such as the expansion of professional occupations that opens up new positions for people to move into, regardless of individual effort.

In practice, the two are deeply connected. Increasing variability in intra-generational career paths can also mask or distort trends in inter-generational mobility, making it harder to assess whether opportunity has genuinely expanded across generations.

Education as the engine of mobility in industrial societies

In pre-industrial societies, social position was largely fixed at birth. Feudal hierarchies, caste systems, and inherited land tied individuals to the status of their parents with very little room to move. Industrial societies changed this. With industrialisation, the demand for a technologically skilled labour force began to undermine rigid class distinctions, and education emerged as a key mechanism for social mobility.

The shift is captured well in what sociologists call the industrialism thesis: as societies modernise, the link between social origins and educational attainment weakens, while the link between educational qualifications and eventual occupational destination strengthens. In this framework, educational expansion is seen as a necessary response to the functional demands of an increasingly meritocratic industrial society, where positions need to be filled by qualified individuals rather than simply by those born into the right family.

Credentials and qualifications as mobility tools

Most studies in the United States and other industrialised nations have found educational attainment to be among the most important determinants of occupational and status attainment. Formal qualifications serve as signals to employers about a candidate’s skills and potential – a mechanism known as credentialism. Educational credentials function as a screening device, facilitating access to higher-level occupations and better pay.

This has had a profound effect on intra-generational trajectories. Gaining additional qualifications mid-career is now a recognised pathway for upward movement within a single lifetime. The expansion of higher education has also widened inter-generational mobility by enabling first-generation university students – those whose parents never attended university – to access occupational classes previously beyond their reach.

A major cross-national study published in the American Sociological Review, covering nearly 300 societal contexts across four continents, found that greater participation in post-secondary education is consistently associated with higher levels of inter-generational social mobility. Crucially, the study found that educational expansion both equalises access to higher education and reduces the direct transmission of socioeconomic advantage among those who reach similar education levels.

The limits of education as a leveller

Education is not a simple equaliser, however. Education can be seen as a double-edged sword: it opens pathways to mobility for those who cannot inherit privilege, but it also contributes to the transmission of inequality between generations, since access to quality education is itself unevenly distributed across social strata. Children from affluent families attend better-resourced schools, receive more academic support, and are more likely to reach elite institutions.

French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu highlighted the role of cultural capital – the knowledge, language, and dispositions that families pass on to children – in determining educational success. Children from professional-class households often arrive at school already familiar with the cultural codes rewarded in academic settings, giving them a built-in advantage that has nothing to do with raw ability. Rising tuition fees, increasing school segregation, and growing economic inequality all make it harder for students from less-resourced families to succeed academically, limiting the equalising power of education.

Open and closed societies: meritocracy versus reproduction

The debate over social mobility ultimately comes down to a tension between two competing visions of society. The meritocratic ideal holds that positions should be distributed based on talent and effort. In a meritocratic system, individuals are rewarded for their achievements, and social mobility is seen as a reflection of individual effort. Critics, however, argue that this framework personalises success and failure while ignoring structural conditions.

Social mobility is not solely determined by individual merit – it is also influenced by factors such as social class, race, and gender. Conflict theorists argue that education and cultural capital frequently reproduce inequality rather than dismantling it, since those in positions of power have the resources to ensure their children maintain advantage across generations. Research drawing on 25 years of data from 23 countries found that people living in more unequal societies tend to attribute success to individual hard work, underestimating the structural barriers that shape life outcomes – a finding that points to how inequality can sustain itself through beliefs as much as through material conditions.

High levels of social mobility – whether intra- or inter-generational – tend to generate social optimism and economic productivity, as people believe that effort genuinely pays off. Low mobility, by contrast, breeds frustration and entrenches disadvantage, particularly when inherited privilege quietly determines outcomes that appear to reflect individual achievement.

What do you think? Is education truly the great equaliser in modern societies, or does it primarily reinforce the advantages of those who are already privileged? And when we celebrate stories of individuals who “made it” through hard work and qualifications, are we recognising real openness in the class structure – or are we overlooking the structural barriers that still determine who gets the chance to try?
